Species Distribution Modeling in New South Wales

Comparing XGBoost, MaxEnt and hierarchical Bayesian models on the same geographically held-out survey records—and translating predictions into practical rare-taxon survey priorities.

Code & verified resultsAll projects

At a glance

1,167 forest plots · 8 anonymized taxa · 9,336 plot–taxon observations · 5 geographic folds · 23 matched taxon–fold evaluations

1. Where and how were models validated?

Public disdat NSW open-forest survey data. Each plot and all its taxon records stay together in one held-out fold. A two-dimensional 4×4 quantile grid assigns entire cells to five folds. The map uses actual survey coordinates and geographic boundaries.

2. What did the matched comparison find?

The same 23 eligible taxon–fold test records were used to compare three implemented pipelines across five evaluable taxa.

Model pipelineMean ROC AUCKey distinction
Pooled XGBoost0.8016Boosted trees; environmental and taxon-identity inputs
Species-specific MaxEnt0.7865Regularized environmental suitability
Hierarchical Bayesian0.7610Taxon-level partial pooling

Key result: XGBoost exceeded MaxEnt by a mean paired AUC of +0.0151. This is a modest descriptive advantage, not proof that boosted trees outperform other SDM algorithms in general: the pipelines use different predictor information and score interpretations.

3. What changes the interpretation?

Feature representation: Fold-local categorical encoding produced similar full-feature XGBoost discrimination. Removing taxon identity sharply reduced pooled-model performance, but also changed the prediction task; it cannot be interpreted as a pure encoding effect.

Model selection: The archived spatial XGBoost benchmark used a fixed 300-round configuration, not nested spatial hyperparameter tuning. Bayesian convergence checks passed in independent longer-chain fits, while the matched comparison uses archived original-fit predictions.

4. Bayesian model-specification robustness

We extended the hierarchical Bayesian pipeline with quadratic rainfall and temperature responses and a separate nonpooled specification. The extensions were assessed using the same five geographic folds—not a second independent dataset. AUC/AP are defined for 30 taxon–fold cells in the expanded Bayesian analysis; the original three-pipeline comparison uses 23 matched cells.

Probability quality: Pooled out-of-fold Brier score improved from 0.06084 to 0.05404, and 10-bin expected calibration error from 0.01985 to 0.01255. The mean five-nearest-neighbor residual Moran's I fell from 0.341 to 0.291, although substantial positive spatial autocorrelation remained. These are internal spatial-holdout diagnostics, not external geographic validation.

Influence check: Excluding taxon nsw18, the quadratic model retained a mean paired AUC gain of +0.0286 and AP gain of +0.0663. Improvements were heterogeneous across taxa. The nonpooled variant did not show comparable overall predictive gains, despite improved top-25 retrieval for one rare taxon.

Independent refitting under a second spatial partition: We generated 15 outcome-blind geographic clusters, allocated them to five new folds, and refitted both linear and quadratic hierarchical Bayesian models (10 successful jobs). Across 34 evaluable taxon–fold cells, quadratic terms improved mean paired ROC AUC by +0.0168 and average precision by +0.0230; across all 40 cells, mean paired Brier decreased by 0.0065. Pooled ROC AUC increased from 0.8856 to 0.8999, and pooled average precision from 0.3360 to 0.4087. This confirms favorable average results under an alternative fold geometry within the same dataset, not external transfer. Second-partition verified results. A subsequent MCMC and calibration audit confirmed all convergence thresholds (max Rhat 1.0082; no divergences), lower pooled ECE10 (Δ −0.00581), and lower mean held-out residual Moran's I (0.2815 to 0.2471). Improvements were heterogeneous: Brier improved for 5 of 8 anonymous taxa, concentrated in nsw18 and nsw24, and positive residual spatial autocorrelation remained.

Validation limitation: The 30 expanded taxon–fold cells overlap with the original 23 and cannot be counted as an independent replication. Additional spatial partitioning or independent-region validation remains future work. Verified extension results and methods.

5. Can predictions make rare-taxon surveys more efficient?

The practical question: When a taxon has too few detections for reliable fold-level ROC AUC, can a model rank a limited number of plots to recover more of the recorded detections than random selection? This is a retrospective, fixed-budget prioritization test—not a new occupancy model or a prospective field trial.

For each of the five geographic folds, the Bayesian model ranked the held-out plots. We selected the top 10, 25 or 50 per fold and counted recorded detections captured. The random benchmark uses each fold's actual prevalence and the same screening budget. A top-25 strategy screens 125 of 1,167 plots (10.7%) across folds.

Interpretation: The ranking concentrated known detections in a small fraction of the surveyed plots, particularly for nsw20. But nsw19 recovered only 3 of 8 detections at the same budget. With just 6–18 positives per taxon, each detection materially changes the percentage; these are descriptive results, not robust estimates of future detection rates.

What this means for conservation teams: A ranked shortlist could help plan targeted resurvey visits when budgets are limited. Before field deployment, the model needs independent prospective validation, imperfect-detection assessment, accessibility/cost constraints and checks that selected plots cover the landscape rather than clustering in one area. High retrospective enrichment is not evidence of higher occupancy or confirmed field-survey yield.
