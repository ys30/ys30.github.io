When Climate Stresses Meet Grid Constraints

I built an auditable Python/PyPSA workflow to test how simultaneous heat and low-wind conditions interact with grid resource assumptions—and why the resources included in a model can materially change its conclusions.

Business question: can we trust climate-risk scores without checking model assumptions?

Energy teams use simulation to compare heatwaves, renewable shortfalls and resilience investments. I tested a crucial failure mode: even with the same weather, changing the available generation representation can change the size and spatial location of a compound-stress signal. That matters when a scenario analysis is used to prioritize infrastructure, flexibility or further due diligence.

What I built

An end-to-end workflow joining agency-reported hourly electricity demand, meteorological inputs and an explicitly partial generator inventory; validating time conventions and source categories; and running matched four-case heat × wind optimization experiments under two illustrative network configurations and three assumed levels of extra firm thermal capacity.

  • Ingestion and provenance: audited hour-ending versus hour-start reporting; matched 2024 EIA-923 generator records with EIA-860 nameplate data; preserved missing source categories rather than assuming zero.
  • Optimization: modeled baseline, heat-only, low-wind-only and combined stress in PyPSA using HiGHS; separately compared isolated regions with a three-link illustrative network.
  • Robustness: repeated scenarios across four adjacent 168-hour windows, 0% / +25% / +50% hypothetical capacity, and a distinct 2023 source-input window.
  • Engineering controls: retained data lineage, solver outputs, checksum evidence and CI audit reports; distinguished observed data, stipulated assumptions and optimized outputs.

The decision-relevant result: reserve margins shape compound-stress signals

Without added capacity, modeled system-level heat–wind second differences were 8.6–25.2 GWh across eight week/network cases. An assumed capacity increase reduced the absolute interaction in seven cases, but not all: in Week 2 under illustrative links, the result rose from 16.2 to 18.6 GWh at +25% capacity. The signed relative interaction increased in all eight cases when moving from 0% to +25%.

Business value: resource-aware climate scenario screening

The transferable deliverable is an evidence-gated scenario-testing framework: first validate source timestamps and coverage; then label structural assumptions; compare scenarios on multiple resource configurations; audit model sensitivities; and show decision-makers which conclusions survive those changes. The same workflow patterns apply to climate-tech analytics, grid planning support, renewable portfolio screening and model-validation pipelines.

Scope and model-risk disclosure. The core model intentionally represents generic thermal and wind generation, not a full operating fleet. Hydropower, solar, nuclear and storage are not separately included in the main factorial optimization; network transfer limits and heat/wind response functions are illustrative. Computed shortages are conditional screening outputs—not actual outages, EENS, calibrated BA adequacy or future blackout predictions. The separate Grand Coulee model adds assumed hydro and storage and must not be conflated with the four-week model.
