When Does Adverse Weather Matter for Pedestrian Injury Severity?

A five-year, Great Britain–wide STATS19 study examining how adverse weather and rural versus urban road environments jointly relate to the severity of reported pedestrian injuries. Includes GIS mapping, logistic and multinomial modeling, marginal standardization, and selection-bias sensitivity analyses.

Key finding

Adverse weather did not uniformly increase injury severity among reported pedestrian casualties. The adjusted weather association was near zero on urban roads (OR 1.002, 95% CI 0.956–1.049), but positive on rural roads (OR 1.274, 95% CI 1.124–1.444). The weather × rural interaction was OR 1.272 (95% CI 1.114–1.453).

Absolute risk difference

Standardized predictions indicated an adverse-weather-related change in KSI probability of +0.03 percentage points in urban settings and +5.13 points in rural settings, a rural–urban difference of +5.10 points (95% CI 2.27–8.00).

GIS & statistical analysis

Mapped local-authority KSI, adverse-weather and rural-road proportions across Great Britain (manuscript Figure 3). Linked casualty and collision records without duplicating casualties in multi-vehicle collisions; fitted logistic, multinomial and threshold-specific severity models with upstream road-context covariates and weather-by-rural effect modification.

Selection bias & robustness

Examined police-attendance restriction, inverse-probability weighting, official severity adjustments, road-surface and behavioral covariates, police-force mixed effects and a vehicle-occupant comparator. The interaction attenuated in police-attended-only data (OR 1.168) but was restored by attendance weighting (OR 1.265). Residual unmeasured selection remains possible.

Practical relevance

The findings demonstrate how integrated operational records, geographic context, interpretable modeling and data-quality checks can identify road environments warranting further safety assessment. They describe injury severity conditional on a reported casualty, not crash incidence or a causal weather effect.
