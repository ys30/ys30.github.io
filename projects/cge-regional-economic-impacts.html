From direct damage to economy-wide effects

This study extends the sector-specific damage-function work into three regional recursive-dynamic CGE models. Climate shocks enter through seven active channels and propagate through production, household demand, prices, trade, capital accumulation and labor, making indirect effects visible rather than treating direct monetary damage as the endpoint.

Regional disparity

Under SSP5-8.5 in 2050, modeled real-GDP effects reach −1.28% in Beijing, −3.45% in Tianjin and −5.44% in Hebei. Across all six climate models, the ordering Hebei > Tianjin > Beijing remains stable even though the magnitude varies.

What drives the losses?

Leave-one-channel-out decomposition identifies heat-related labor workability as the dominant modeled pathway, accounting for roughly 79–95% of climate losses. Under SSP5-8.5, cumulative GDP losses are 0.98%, 2.38% and 3.67% in Beijing, Tianjin and Hebei; labor contributes 0.84, 2.25 and 3.17 percentage points respectively.

Sector and price propagation

The CGE layer reveals responses that direct damage functions alone cannot show. For example, under SSP5-8.5 in 2050, modeled water-sector output falls while output prices rise, reflecting economy-wide adjustment through interconnected markets and resource constraints.

Policy experiment

Growth-oriented fiscal, investment and productivity experiments can substantially raise economic output, but they do not materially eliminate the proportional climate loss. The result separates economic growth from targeted climate adaptation: stronger baseline growth is not itself a substitute for reducing climate vulnerability.

Interactive product · next

The existing CGE Shiny dashboard will be rebuilt against the v33 results as a Climate Economy Explorer, with dedicated views for GDP trajectories, damage-channel decomposition, sector impacts, climate-model uncertainty and policy experiments.

Research chain

Sector-Specific Climate Damage Functions quantifies the direct physical and sectoral shocks. This CGE study carries those shocks forward into the wider economy, providing a connected physical-risk → sector-damage → macroeconomic-impact workflow.
